Teaching

I want students to question a result, interrogate an assumption, and reason about a system rather than simply operate it, a skill I treat as inseparable from using AI responsibly.

Education and teaching hold a special place in my heart. I have spent the last two years as a full-time, teaching-focused faculty member at the University at Albany's Massry School of Business, where I designed and taught four courses for the AI for Business program, spanning the undergraduate-to-graduate range, in person and online. I do not see teaching as separate from my research: explaining a concept to students with very different backgrounds forces a depth of understanding that writing a paper alone does not, and I approach it the way I approach my research, iteratively, and with the people I am designing for.

My teaching began in 2021, when I received the Graduate Teaching Fellowship from Penn State's College of Information Sciences and Technology, a competitive award given to two or three students a year, and independently designed and taught IST 331, Foundations of Human-Centered Design. That first semester was upended when student covid cases forced the course into hybrid mid-semester, and I learned to treat a syllabus as something I revise in response to my students, not something I set once and follow.

How I teach

I have students learn by doing, on problems that are deliberately unscoped the way real problems are. In Programming for Analytics, students build up from Python fundamentals to a mini-project analyzing real Reddit data with sentiment analysis, on questions they formulate themselves. In Tools for AI and Data Analytics, I structure the semester around the lifecycle of a data analytics project and then hand ownership to students through a group project on a dataset of their choice. In Advanced Data Analytics, teams critically evaluate published machine learning research. In my AI ethics course, students may choose any medium for their final project, and the results have included zines, working sculptures, and short films alongside papers.

I do not ban generative AI; I teach it. Students may use LLMs for learning and reference but must justify their decisions, and I ask them to write honestly about where AI misled them. Several have described trusting an AI-generated result until it stopped matching the data, then revising their approach, which is exactly the shift from accepting an output to interrogating it that I want every course to build.

I am committed to classrooms where students from every discipline and background can engage. I rely on the Feynman technique and multiple modalities so that students with advanced programming backgrounds and students from non-STEM fields can both succeed, and I mentor students from underrepresented groups in STEM, drawing on my own experience as an immigrant woman of color. I have mentored nine students, four doctoral, two master's, and three undergraduate, on research, data analysis, and technical writing.
