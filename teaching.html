Teaching

Teaching students how to think, not just what to run

My goal in any classroom is the same: question a result, interrogate an assumption, reason about a system rather than simply operate it. That matters most right now in AI and data-analytics courses, where it's tempting to let the tool do the thinking.

I don't ban generative AI in my courses; banning it just pushes disengaged students toward misuse rather than away from it. Instead, I build in structure: students may use AI for learning but must justify every decision, and a reflection assignment asking students to write honestly about where AI misled them has produced some of the most useful teaching feedback I've gotten. Every policy is deliberately different across courses, because an introductory programming class and a graduate AI ethics seminar demand a different relationship to the tool.

I'm currently extending this into department-level work: redesigning assessments (oral exams, portfolios, paired in- and out-of-class work) to be more resilient to AI, and building AI literacy as a throughline across a curriculum rather than a single ethics course.
